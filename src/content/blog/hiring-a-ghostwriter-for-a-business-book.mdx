There's a common picture of hiring a ghostwriter: you hand over the idea, and a few months later a finished book arrives. Liz Heflin, who ghostwrites nonfiction business books, says that's not how it works, and it's not how she'd want it to.

I'm writing a book myself, so I had my own reasons for asking. A friend who's spent years on a self-help book told me straight away to work with a ghostwriter: writing is a skill, and it doesn't make sense to spend years learning it. I wanted to know what that would actually involve. Liz walked me through when it makes sense, where your time goes, and how she makes a book sound like the person whose name is on it.

## When does hiring a ghostwriter make sense?

Liz sees two main groups. The first is obvious: people who don't have the time. That's usually founders and executives, people without the bandwidth to write a book on top of everything else.

The second is less obvious. These are people with a story that matters deeply to them, who want it out in the world in the most polished form possible, and who don't feel equipped to write it themselves.

> "You're not just getting somebody to put words on the page. More often than not, it's very much a collaboration. You get somebody to bounce ideas off of, and to help shape and hone the narrative arc, what the story should be, and what your goals are on the back end."

She also added a third group: people who write well but hate doing it. If you know you'd dread every minute, that's a fair reason to bring someone in.

## The ghostwriter is also your accountability partner

One thing Liz kept coming back to is momentum. Plenty of people have been "writing a book" for five years. A ghostwriter sets a timeline and keeps everyone on it.

How involved you are is a spectrum. Some authors want to write a lot of it themselves and just need help polishing and deciding which stories belong. Others say writing isn't their thing, here are the ideas, please craft it. Both work.

I'd add that paying for it changes things too. A friend can ask where your chapter is. When you've paid someone to help you finish, the deadline means more.

## How much time does a ghostwritten book still take?

This is the misconception Liz most wants to correct. Hiring a ghostwriter doesn't mean washing your hands of the project. She runs it in four stages, and the author is involved in every one.

1. **Strategy.** The goals of the book, the audience, the tone. Usually a couple of hours of calls.
2. **Interviews.** For a full-length book, she tells clients to plan for 10 to 30 hours of interviews spread over several months. That gives her enough material for a book in the 40,000 to 70,000 word range.
3. **Writing.** The stage where the author can put their feet up the most. Even so, they read each chapter as it's drafted, to catch anything off course early.
4. **Editing.** Two full rounds, then a final proofread. How long this takes varies too much to put a number on.

> "It usually takes about six months to one year from kickoff to manuscript in hand. So you are working very, very closely with your ghostwriter for a good chunk of time."

## Ghostwriter, book coach, or writing it yourself?

Liz's advice is to ask yourself some honest questions, starting with: do I have the writing skills to do this story justice?

- **If the answer is no**, a ghostwriter makes the most sense. Otherwise it's hard to get the book to the standard you want.
- **If you're comfortable with the writing** but less sure about the ideas, the structure, or who the book is for, a book coach is a good fit. A coach is also the one asking where the chapters are.
- **If you have the time and the skills**, write it yourself. Doing the writing teaches you things about your own subject that you won't get any other way.

Whichever route you pick, she says, nobody writes a book alone:

> "You could be the best writer in the world. Everybody benefits from talking through their ideas and having a sounding board. Writing feels like it happens in isolation, but it's really quite a community-based endeavor."

## Where a business book actually pays off

Most of Liz's clients write business books to bring in work: a coach who wants higher-value clients, a consultant who wants to get on stages and give keynotes. The cover price is the smallest part of the picture.

> "There's a million and one ways that you can monetize a book, and book sales are, counterintuitively, the tiniest little slice of that puzzle. You're usually seeing the returns on the offer that's being built and promoted on the back end of the book."

Some of her clients simply hand out copies at events. The value isn't the $20 a copy brings in. It's the conversations and the trust that come with being a published author.

## How does a ghostwriter capture your voice?

It's the question everyone asks, and Liz's answer starts with the interviews. She usually meets clients once a week for two-hour sessions over two to four months. Those calls do double duty: she gathers the content, and she listens to how the person thinks, how they connect ideas, and which words they use and never use.

She's also honest that the first draft won't be perfect:

> "As deeply as you listen, as empathetically as you listen, you're never going to 100% nail it the first time. Nobody can, and it's disingenuous to say that you're going to. But that's what the editing phase is for."

And the aim isn't to copy how you sound on a call. It's to put the best version of you on the page: still recognizably you, with more polish. Tone and words to avoid get agreed in the strategy stage, then refined through the edits until it's right.

## FAQ

**When does it make sense to hire a ghostwriter?**
Two cases stand out. You're short on time, as most founders and executives are. Or the story matters a lot to you and you don't think you can do it justice on the page yourself. If you can write but hate doing it, that counts too.

**How much time does a ghostwritten book still take?**
More than most people expect. Liz plans for a few hours of strategy calls, 10 to 30 hours of interviews over several months, reviewing chapters as they're written, and two full rounds of edits. Expect six months to a year from kickoff to finished manuscript.

**What's the difference between a ghostwriter and a book coach?**
A ghostwriter does the writing with you. A book coach helps you write it yourself: shaping the ideas, the structure, and the strategy, and keeping you accountable. If you're comfortable with the writing but unsure of the story, a coach is often the better fit.

**Will a ghostwritten book sound like me?**
It should, but not on the first draft. Liz listens for how you think and the words you use during the interviews, then the editing rounds are where you flag anything you'd never say. The goal is the polished version of you, not a transcript.

## What this means if you're planning a book

Whether you hire a ghostwriter, a coach, or write it yourself, plan for the book to take real time, and line up people who'll give you honest feedback along the way. A ghostwriter saves you the writing. They don't save you from showing up.
