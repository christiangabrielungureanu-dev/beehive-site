My agency is called Beehive. Philip Atkinson is a beekeeper who runs a leadership agency called Hive Logic and wrote a book called *Bee Wise: 12 Leadership Lessons from a Busy Beehive*. I had to have him on.

What makes the book worth talking about isn't only the bees. Philip wrote 12 chapters, then invited 12 experts to reply to them, so the book became a conversation rather than a lecture. A year after launch, I wanted to know why he wrote a leadership book this way, whether a playful theme cost him credibility, and what the book has done for his business.

## Why use bees to write about leadership?

Hive Logic works with the leadership teams of multinationals, NGOs, banks, and startups, and *Bee Wise* is, in Philip's words, the work the agency does. The chapters cover decision making, communication, creativity, organizational design, and more.

The bees started as an icebreaker. In meetings at the multinationals where he used to work, Philip would open by talking about what the bees were doing. Then he noticed it changed the conversation. He was once brought in to resolve a conflict in a team, and instead of starting with the conflict, they talked about how bees resolve theirs.

> "It just softened the conversation, it made people interested. It stopped people fighting for a moment."

The point isn't to be like bees. Some of the lessons are about what not to copy:

> "A bee in summer, the life expectancy is thirty-four days. They work nonstop, they never sleep, they never stop, and then they die. So a big theme from the book is: let's not be busy as bees."

## Does a theme cost a leadership book its credibility?

This is the worry I hear from a lot of experts. When your buyers are senior leaders, it feels like the book has to be serious.

Philip's answer: there are plenty of serious, technical leadership books that nobody reads. *Bee Wise* is fully referenced and grounded in academic and management research. The theme is what gets people to pick it up. A hive of 70,000 bees making a decision is an easy way into how 70,000 people in an organization make one. Bees communicating in the dark, under pressure, in the heat, opens up a conversation about what else is going on when someone sends an email at work.

> "We do the hard stuff with an interesting edge to it. That's what we do."

The book reads the way his agency works. That's a big part of why it works as a business tool.

## Why 12 experts replied to his chapters

After writing the first chapter, on organizational design, Philip stopped. Why had he written it alone, when he never works alone? He talked it over with a coach and a friend, and they suggested sending the chapter to an expert in the field and asking them to reply.

> "All of a sudden it becomes a dialogue. And by becoming a dialogue, it becomes deeper and more resonant than just from me."

Every chapter now has a reply from a guest expert. The first, from a partner at Ernst & Young, opens with: "I don't agree with Philip." Philip was delighted. He didn't want agreement; he wanted a discussion.

He also admits it was terrifying. Sharing unfinished work with 12 experts is a very exposed thing to do. But readers have told him they've never seen it done in a leadership book, and it lifted the whole thing. As someone writing my own book, I took note: writing alone means you only get one brain.

## Where the idea came from: Friday is Hive Day

Every Friday, Philip writes a LinkedIn post called "Friday is Hive Day": a few facts about bees, used as a metaphor for working life. The posts kept getting a lot of interest, and he realized the same model could carry a book. In a sense, *Bee Wise* is a longer form of those posts.

He also just likes writing. Working something through on the page improves the quality of his thinking, and he'd always wanted to write a book. From there, it was a matter of choosing 12 meaningful topics and turning each into a chapter. The book follows the four seasons across 12 months, from beginnings and growth through closing, learning, and consolidating.

## What the book has opened up, a year on

*Bee Wise* launched on World Bee Day and has just turned one. The paperback sells on Amazon, particularly well in the US, and a hardback edition is sold through the book's own site.

All proceeds go to Bees for Development, a charity that helps people in developing countries build beekeeping businesses. The book has already raised over £10,000 for it.

For Hive Logic, the book works as what Philip calls "a wonderful, fun business card":

- **Clients buy it for their teams.**
- **Keynotes:** organizers buy copies for a whole department or audience when he speaks.
- **Workshops and events:** he uses it as a teaching tool and as a giveaway.

Because it matches the brand and has real depth behind it, it fits naturally into the work rather than sitting beside it.

## The best surprise: readers talking to each other

What Philip loves most is when two people who've read the book start talking about it, and a year on, it keeps happening. Recently, someone he didn't know posted on LinkedIn that the book had blown their mind. People they didn't know replied that they'd read it too, and a conversation about how a beehive makes decisions took off. Philip just sat back and watched.

> "The goal of the book was actually to help people think a bit harder about topics, which is working. And what I do with my work is build communities, and it's working."

## How he launched it, and what he'd do differently

Philip worked with [Kory Kirby](/blog/business-book-that-grows-your-business), who led the design and production of the book and published it. He followed Kory's advice: build a community of readers before launch, send advance copies, and encourage people to write about it and share it.

Then came two launch events, in London and in Switzerland, with about a hundred people at each. The launches weren't about the book, and that was deliberate. Guest writers ran interactive workshops on themes from the book. Bees communicate in the dark of the hive through a "waggle dance", so the London party was held at a ballet studio, where a choreographer and a musician led everyone through an experience of nonverbal communication.

> "It was exactly what we talk about in the book, without actually holding the book up and reading from it. I want people to experience it."

His one regret is that he was too soft with his pre-readers. He'd now ask them more explicitly to leave reviews on Amazon. The book has plenty of good ones, but he'd like to put more energy into what he calls "the Amazon marketing machine".

That's a common gap, and an easy one to close: when you send an advance copy, say exactly what you'd like the reader to do with it, and when.

## FAQ

**Does a playful theme hurt a leadership book's credibility?**
Not if the substance is there. *Bee Wise* uses bees as a way into each topic, but every chapter is fully referenced and answered by a guest expert. Philip's view is that the theme is what gets people to pick the book up and read it, which most technical leadership books never manage.

**Why invite other experts to reply to your chapters?**
It turns the book into a dialogue. Philip felt uncomfortable writing alone when he never works alone, so he asked an expert to reply to each of the 12 chapters. The first reply opens with "I don't agree with Philip," and he was delighted: the disagreement makes the book deeper.

**What can a leadership book do for a consulting business?**
For Philip it works as a business card with depth. Clients buy copies for their teams, organizers buy copies for the audience when he gives a keynote, and he uses it in workshops and at events.

**What would he do differently at launch?**
Ask pre-readers more explicitly for Amazon reviews. The advance copies, community building, and launch events all worked, but he feels he was too soft about asking for reviews.

## What this means if you're planning a book

Philip's book works because it's an extension of how he already works: the Friday posts, the bee metaphors in workshops, the habit of bringing other experts in. If you're planning a leadership book, look at what already gets people talking in your work. That's probably your book.
