When a friend asked Tom Stearns whether he'd ever write a book, his answer was roughly: I don't know. A lot of business books are a bit boring, and what would I have to say that's new? A year later the same friend, Peter Cleary, came back with an idea: what if they wrote a graphic novel together?

That's how *Graphic Sales* started. It's part comic book, part playbook for sales leaders building a team of prospectors. Tom runs a consultancy that helps B2B companies fix and scale their sales teams, and I wanted to know three things: why a comic, what the book was meant to do for his business, and how he got it in front of people.

## Why write a business book as a comic?

The book didn't start as a book. For about two years, Tom and Peter wrote short comics, two or three pages each, roughly one a month. They brought in illustrator Eli Stone, who draws and colors everything by hand, and published the stories free on LinkedIn, on their site, and to subscribers.

The book came when Tom noticed a pattern in his consulting work. He kept writing the same kind of document for clients: a sales playbook, covering who the ideal customer is, which channels to use, and real examples of what to say.

> "I said to Peter, what we could do right now is write a playbook on how to write a playbook. I think this would be a useful resource, and we've got a lot of comics already written that support that."

The comics do a specific job. Every story is true, usually a past disaster of Tom's or Peter's, and each chapter opens with one: what not to do. The writing that follows shows what to do instead.

> "People feel stories. They remember stories. It makes it much more compelling than just telling people what to do. They can feel it."

## What surprised him after the launch

About two months after launch, the thing that pleased Tom most was what readers said, including people he'd never met. He's read plenty of business books he had to force himself to finish: repetitive, slow, worth it for one good idea. He wanted the opposite: a book that moves, that you come back to, and that makes you laugh.

The reviews told him it worked. Readers said they'd finish a chapter, plan to put it down, see another comic on the next page, and end up four or five chapters further in.

## It was never about book sales

Tom thought hard about what success would look like before writing anything.

> "For a business like mine, it's wise to be deliberate in what the outcome should be. It was never book sales."

Most of his work comes through his network: people hire him, hire him again, or refer him. The book is another way to stay in front of that network, and a way to show how he works: strategic, but deep in the details, with example after example from real clients.

There was also an unexpected benefit, which another author pointed out to him. When a new client has read the book, they've already seen the blueprint for the work. Tom can point to a chapter and say: that's what we're doing next. It makes the engagement more efficient.

Speaking was a longer-term goal. It arrived sooner than he expected: a client asked him to speak at their sales kickoff and bought copies for the whole company. He's also been introduced to new people through the book, and a project he'd been working on for a couple of years finally closed. He's not sure how much the book had to do with that last one, but he thinks it helped.

## How he marketed the launch

Tom's launch had four parts:

1. **Advance copies to a tiered list.** On the advice of [Kory Kirby](/blog/business-book-that-grows-your-business), who helped them publish, Tom built a list in tiers: longtime friends and colleagues in the industry, influential people in his space, then clients and investors he works with. Each tier was asked to do something different: post about it on LinkedIn, buy a copy, or leave a review.
2. **A launch event** for friends, family, local contacts, and potential clients.
3. **A long build-up on LinkedIn.** Tom already posted regularly, so he wrote about the ideas in the book for months beforehand, without making it a sales pitch.
4. **Podcasts.** He hired a small agency to book him as a guest, and that's still going.

That third point is the one I'd underline. Tom didn't start from zero on launch day. He'd been building an audience on LinkedIn for years, so when the book arrived, people were already listening.

## What comes next

Tom plans to keep doing podcasts, and he's thinking about entering the book for awards, comic awards included, since the format makes it stand out. He and Peter are also writing new comics again, to publish on LinkedIn and point readers back to the book.

My suggestion to him: every podcast he does is also content. Short clips from an interview work well on LinkedIn or in a newsletter, so each conversation keeps working after it airs.

## Advice for consultants a year out from their first book

Tom's main advice is to take the publishing decision seriously, and give it time.

> "You're going to get approached by companies that say they can do it for you, and it's a lot of money. So do your homework."

There are big publishers, smaller ones, ghostwriters, and doing it yourself, which means dealing with ISBNs, barcodes, and copyright. Tom spent about a year working out how to publish before he started writing. He and Peter didn't need help with the writing; they needed strategic and practical help, and they wanted their budget to go on the illustrator.

So start with three questions: who the book is for, what you bring that's different, and what you want the book to do. For consultants, he says, the answer is rarely book sales.

> "It was a ton of work, and it feels really good. I'm glad I did it. But doing anything well takes time, energy, a lot of thinking, and good people around you."

## FAQ

**Can a business book work as a comic?**
Tom's experience says yes. Each chapter of *Graphic Sales* opens with a true story of what not to do, drawn as a comic, followed by the playbook for doing it right. Readers told him the stories kept them turning pages, which is the problem most business books have.

**What should a consultant's book do for their business?**
Decide before you write. For Tom it was never book sales: it was about strengthening his reputation in a network that already sends him work, showing how he thinks, and opening the door to speaking. A clear goal shapes every choice after it.

**How did Tom market the launch?**
Advance copies to a tiered list of contacts, each tier asked to do something different; a launch event; a long build-up of LinkedIn posts about the book's ideas; and guest spots on podcasts.

**How early should you start planning a business book?**
Earlier than you think. Tom spent about a year before writing just working out how to publish it: big publishers, smaller ones, ghostwriters, or doing it yourself. Each costs something different, and the right one depends on the help you actually need.

## What this means if you're planning a book

Tom's launch worked because most of it was in place before the book existed: years of LinkedIn posts, a network that already knew him, and a clear idea of what the book was for. If your book is a year away, that's the part to start on now.
