Most of my conversations on this show are about using a book to grow a business. Ra'anan Cohen isn't selling anything. He built two tech companies, sold the second one, Bringg, after it became a unicorn, and wrote a book for himself and his kids. He never meant to publish it.

The book, *Confessions of a Unicorn Founder*, spent over a year on Israel's bestseller list. Ra'anan didn't spend a single dollar on promotion, and he turned down the biggest in-store promotion in the country. I wanted to understand how a book finds its readers when nobody is pushing it, and why he cared so much about finding the right readers rather than more of them.

## A book he never meant to publish

Ra'anan's career in tech was a roller coaster. He came in with no tech background, built a telecom company and took it public, then watched it crash eight years later. At 40, with four kids and a mortgage, he started again. The second company grew far bigger, survived an attempt by its investors to push him out, and in 2021 he sold.

His life went from 200 miles an hour to zero over one weekend. On the Monday after signing, the only email in his inbox was from his child's third-grade teacher. So he flew to Bali for a month, fasted, meditated, and wrote a bucket list for the next part of his life. Near the top: write the story.

He spent a year writing it, then gave it to a few friends. One of them, without asking, sent the manuscript to Israel's biggest publisher. Ra'anan called to apologize and ask them to delete it. Ten people at the publisher had already read it, and they asked him in for coffee. He told them it wasn't a commercial book; he planned to give it away to people who might benefit from it.

> "They said, go check out our website. There's like a thousand free books. Did you ever download a free book? No one will read it. Your entire mission will go to waste."

He signed on the spot.

## Low expectations, then a bestseller

The publisher was careful to set his expectations. Around 8,000 new books come out in Israel each year, and 95% of them don't sell even 500 copies. He was an unknown author.

From the third week, the book was on the bestseller list, and it stayed there for more than a year. Israel's top university built a mandatory business administration course around it. It's now translated and on Amazon internationally, and a production company has bought the film rights. Ra'anan's email address is in the book, and readers write to him every day.

## How the book found its readers

When a 12,000-member Facebook group for Israeli authors asked him to explain his marketing strategy, budget, and team, he had to tell them he had none. Preparing for that talk is what made him work out what had actually happened.

At launch, he posted once each on LinkedIn, Instagram, and Facebook. The first readers were the obvious ones: founders and entrepreneurs already connected to him. They liked it, and some of them posted about it on their own. One reader with a podcast invited him on, which led to more podcast invitations. More exposure meant more readers posting, and more posts meant more readers.

Ra'anan calls it product-market fit, the holy grail of startups:

> "It's not enough that my book is in the stores and everyone can see it. I want it to be visible to the right readers. And I want it not to be visible to the readers who will be disappointed."

## The promotion he turned down

Once the book was popular, Israel's biggest bookstore chain offered him its top promotion: his book next to the till in all 200 stores, at 50% off with any other purchase. It's the promotion every author dreams of, and they told him it would multiply his sales.

He said no.

> "I don't want my 70-year-old next-door neighbor, looking for a gift for her niece, to see this book on promotion at the cashier and take it. And then the niece says, it's not my world, I don't really like it."

He wanted it the other way round. People who saw him on LinkedIn or Instagram, or saw a reader post about the book, could decide whether they liked his style. The book is casual, written in the first person, the way he talks. Some would find him too much and skip it. Others would get curious.

> "If it's the right book for a reader, then this is true love. And when there's true love, magic comes."

That's where the hundreds of unpaid reader posts came from. As Ra'anan put it to the authors' group: that's better than paid promoters.

## Why he won't do discounts

For the same reason, Ra'anan is against discounts and coupons. They're the wrong incentive: they bring in people who want a deal, not people who want the book. He wants readers who heard about it from someone they trust, or who found him and got curious.

He thinks it shows in the ratings. His book has the highest rating among Israel's bestsellers, 4.7 out of 5. He doesn't think the other bestsellers are worse books. He thinks they reached more of the wrong readers.

> "In the book world, I believe there's no good or bad. There is product-market fit. The more you get your book in front of the right readers, the higher your average score will be."

I'd add one thing. Philip Atkinson, author of *Bee Wise*, told me his favorite thing a year after launch was [watching readers talk to each other about the book](/blog/writing-a-leadership-book). That only happens when the people reading it are the people it was written for.

## Who the book turned out to be for

The book's Hebrew title is *Just a Little Bit More*, after John D. Rockefeller's answer when he was asked how much money is enough. That's the real subject: the chase that never ends, at any level, and the choice to say "I have enough". Ra'anan learned it the hard way when his first company crashed after he'd turned down offers to sell.

He and the publisher expected the readers to be founders and would-be founders. It went mainstream instead. Young readers use it as a guide to getting into business with no background. People at a turning point in their careers read it because his own tech career only started at 30, after studying accounting. Different readers take different things from it, and he's found that fascinating.

## What the publisher did, and what's on the author

The publisher got the book into every major store, which matters when shelf space is limited. They expected it to sit on the front tables for three months; it's been there for over a year because it keeps selling. They also arranged some of his talks.

But Ra'anan puts it at about 80% the author's job to create the momentum and the brand, and his author friends agree.

> "Before you release the book, start to talk about the subject matter and attract these potential readers. By far the best promotional way is to get a reader that becomes a super fan."

If he were starting again, he'd begin a year or two before launch, the way some of his friends do. If your book is about relationships, go where people talk about relationships: post, make short videos, and become someone interesting on the subject. When the book arrives, there's already an audience to give it momentum.

## Readers look up the author first

Ra'anan's last point is about how people choose books now. When he picks up a book in a shop and reads the back cover, the next thing he does is look the author up on Instagram. If the author is interesting, he buys the book, sometimes after a short podcast or reel that isn't even about the book.

> "Someone who is interesting, no matter what he writes, I would want to get his thoughts. You put your soul into the book."

## FAQ

**How do you find the right readers for your book?**
Let people get to know you before they meet the book. Ra'anan posted, did podcasts, and gave talks, so readers could decide whether they liked how he thinks. The ones who did picked up the book, loved it, and told others. The ones who didn't stayed away, which is also what you want.

**Why would an author turn down a big promotion?**
Because promotion reaches people who aren't a fit. Ra'anan declined a 50%-off spot next to the till in 200 stores. A book bought as an impulse gift by the wrong reader leads to disappointment and weaker reviews, not to word of mouth.

**Should you discount your book to boost sales?**
Ra'anan is against discounts and coupons. They bring in people who want a deal, not people who want the book. He'd rather readers come because someone they trust recommended it or because something he said sparked their curiosity.

**When should you start building an audience for your book?**
A year or two before launch. Ra'anan's advice is to talk about your subject where your future readers already gather, so that when the book comes out there's an audience ready to give it momentum. In his experience, most of that work falls on the author, not the publisher.

## What this means if you're planning a book

More readers isn't the goal. The right readers are. They finish the book, rate it well, and tell the next right reader. The way to reach them is to show up as yourself, early and often, so the people who'd love your book can find you, and the ones who wouldn't can pass.
